🧩 T6 – Consumerism

Having more or being more?

🔹 Idée générale

La société de consommation a apporté confort, progrès et liberté de choix.
Mais elle a aussi engendré surconsommation, frustration, gaspillage et perte de sens.
Aujourd’hui, l’enjeu est de trouver un équilibre entre prospérité et sobriété.

🔹 Idées clés à développer

  1. Origins of consumerism : post-war prosperity, advertising, mass production.
  2. Psychology of consumption : desire, social status, identity.
  3. Environmental impact : waste, planned obsolescence, pollution.
  4. The illusion of happiness : buying as a substitute for meaning.
  5. New trends : minimalism, ethical consumption, second-hand economy.

🔹 Citations intégrées dans des phrases

  • As Tyler Durden says in Fight Club, “The things you own end up owning you” — a brutal summary of how possessions can take control of our lives.
  • Sociologist Zygmunt Bauman argued that we live in a “liquid society” where people are defined not by what they produce, but by what they consume.
  • Thorstein Veblen already noticed in the 19th century that people buy not only for use, but to display status — what he called “conspicuous consumption.”
  • Naomi Klein’s No Logo showed how big brands have replaced religion for many young people: they give a sense of belonging, but also of dependence.
  • As Gandhi warned, “The world has enough for everyone’s need, but not for everyone’s greed” — a timeless truth about human excess.

🔹 Exemples de phrases prêtes à placer

  • We buy things we don’t need, with money we don’t have, to impress people we don’t know.
  • Consumerism promises happiness but delivers emptiness.
  • Advertising doesn’t sell products — it sells dreams and insecurities.
  • Minimalism is not about owning less, it’s about being less owned.
  • Every purchase is a moral choice: behind cheap prices often lie hidden costs.

🔹 Exemple de structure orale

“We live in a society where having often replaces being.
As Bauman explained, identity today depends on what we consume, not who we are.
Films like Fight Club or books like No Logo show that consumption has become a form of addiction — a way to fill the void left by meaning.
The real question is not how to buy better, but how to live differently.”
